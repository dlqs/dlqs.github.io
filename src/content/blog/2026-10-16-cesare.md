In _The Prince_, Machiavelli writes about Cesare Borgia, whose court he had served in as a Florentine diplomat.

Cesare Borgia was a talented Italian statesman/general/warlord during the Renaissance. In 1503, Borgia had already conquered large portions of the Papal States, and was about to round up his conquests with Tuscany, when a series of unfortunate events brought him down.

First Borgia and his father, Pope Alexander VI, attended the same dinner where they both fell extremely ill. Borgia was bedridden, and his father died shortly after. Amidst the chaos, he was unable to consolidate his influence to over the College of Cardinals, the body appointing the next pope.

Though bedridden, Borgia didn't immediately lose all his influence; his loyalists managed to get Pope Pius III, a compromised candidate, elected. However, this proved to be short lived as Pius was already old and ill, and died just two weeks later. (Some of his opponents may have voted for Pius _because_ of that.)

Subsequently, Cardinal Della Rovere approached Borgia with a deal, to get elected in exchange for protecting Borgia. In his weakened state, Borgia accepted, and in 1503, Pope Julius II was elected. 

That however - as Machiavelli calls it - was Borgia's "biggest mistake". In fact, Julius was a bitter enemy of the Borgias, dating all the way back to his rivalry with Alexander VI over the papacy in 1492, and he had _not_ forgetten about it.

Immediately upon election, Julius broke all his promises to Borgia and had him arrested. Borgia subsequently fled to Navarre where he died later in battle.

Machiavelli's lesson was that Borgia should have never let someone with such longstanding greviances against him acquire so much power, regardless of the promises that man may have made:

> "Whoever believes that among great personages new benefits make old injuries be forgotten deceives himself."

Borgia helped make Julius pope, believing that this would secure his loyalty. Instead, he had handed his greatest enemy the power to destroy him.
